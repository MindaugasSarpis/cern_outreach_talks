## Angle

One dot. A particle of five quarks took 51 years to find, so how do you know you have found something nobody has seen? The cover promises the viewer will see how, dot by dot, and will judge it. Every dot is one computed mass (7's bridge). The viewer judges a 140-dot bump and is fooled, as real physicists were in 2003; sees what tens of thousands of dots look like; judges LHCb's 2015 peak, which held; and hears the rule once, in the cover's words. Then the story hands the dots on: the speaker searched for pentaquarks and, in Bonn, prepared the very data the peak rose in; the group now works from Vilnius; those 800 TB are open, with more behind them; a VU bachelor student collected thousands of dots from them and the bump rose in the same place; and the viewer can start at the masterclass. The particle gathers again at the close. The career path is a thread inside the answer, not an appendix: it enters at 11's bridge („Pentakvarkų ieškojau ir aš“), runs through the road, Vilnius, the Bonn data and Dominykas, and ends on the empty places that wait for the viewer.

## Takeaway

A new particle counts only when there are enough dots for chance to be ruled out and every check has been made. The data the pentaquark was first seen in were prepared by the speaker and are open to anyone; a student barely older than the viewer checked the bump in them; the way in starts at school. You need not know yet what you will be: notice what interests you and dig deeper.
